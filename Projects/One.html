Mapping Unequal Heat Exposure on Native Lands

Summary

How has hazardous heat changed across Native American territories, and where are exposure gaps widening? I led computational analysis at SparkLabNYC combining modeled heat, population rasters, and geographic boundaries. The study examines climate trends from 1982–2020 and recent exposure during 2016–2020. It identifies concentrated burdens in Oklahoma and Arizona, rising hazardous-heat days in 177 territories, and widening disparities in parts of Arizona. The work provides a spatial basis for prioritizing cooling, electricity access, and climate adaptation resources.

Why this matters

A state average can hide the heat conditions experienced by individual Native communities. Historical land dispossession and uneven access to cooling make the location of exposure important: adaptation resources need to reach communities facing the greatest burden, rather than being distributed solely according to a broad regional average.

Population-weighted exposure adds another dimension. A territory with many hazardous days and a large population can account for a much greater total burden than a smaller territory with the same climate. Comparing both measures helps identify different adaptation priorities.

My contribution

As a research intern and project lead at SparkLabNYC, I led computational analysis and developed R workflows to process environmental and population datasets. My work included extracting and validating 21 years of WorldPop population rasters, supporting territory-level exposure estimates, and preparing maps and statistical summaries.

The project builds on the WBGT dataset and methodology developed by Cascade Tuholske, with research guidance from Dr. Robbie M. Parks and methodological support from SparkLabNYC. Lincole Jiang supported gridded population raster extraction.

How the analysis connects climate, people, and place

The analysis follows the path from climate grids and population rasters to territory-level exposure, population-weighted burden, and comparisons with surrounding states. Each figure answers a different question, so the maps are presented alongside the calculation they help explain.

1. Align Native land boundaries, climate, and population

Census TIGER/Line boundaries define the geographic units. Climate estimates provide annual hazardous-heat days, while WorldPop rasters support population extraction. I worked in R to process the spatial data, validate population summaries, and connect outputs through territory identifiers.

The climate time series spans 1982–2020. My annual WorldPop extraction covers 2000–2020; population weights used in historical comparisons must be distinguished from the full climate record. Territories crossing state boundaries require explicit weighting to avoid double counting.

2. Map the frequency of hazardous hot-humid days

The primary threshold is modeled daily maximum WBGT above 28°C. Counting exceedance days produces an annual measure of the climate hazard. Averaging 2016–2020 provides the recent-period spatial comparison shown below.

3. Estimate how many people share that exposure

The project multiplies hazardous-heat days by the estimated population on the analyzed lands to obtain person-days. This combines the frequency of hazardous heat with the number of people exposed. Ranking by person-days can reveal priorities that differ from ranking by heat days alone.

Oklahoma and Arizona account for about 92% of estimated hazardous-heat person-days while holding about 77% of the population on the analyzed lands. The denominator is the population included in this study, rather than all Indigenous people nationally.

4. Compare Native lands with surrounding states

For each year, the analysis compares population-weighted hazardous-heat days on Native lands with the relevant state estimate. Subtracting the state value produces a disparity measure: positive values indicate greater exposure on Native lands; negative values indicate lower exposure than the state comparison.

A community can experience many hazardous days without having a positive state-relative gap. Conversely, a widening gap can be important even when its absolute number of hot days is below that of another region. Reading both measures prevents either comparison from hiding the other.

5. Examine long-term change and changing disparities

Linear regressions summarize the direction of change in annual exposure and exposure differences. The two maps below distinguish an increase in hazardous days from an increase in the gap relative to a state. Those outcomes are related, but they are not interchangeable.

The analysis highlights substantial increases in parts of Oklahoma and widening disparities in parts of Arizona. These maps summarize modeled trends; they do not directly measure illness or establish a future forecast.

What the analysis found

Heat burden is geographically concentrated

The Southwest and Southern Plains show the greatest modeled hazardous-heat exposure. Some Arizona territories exceed 100 hazardous days annually, while many Oklahoma territories exceed 60–80 days in the recent-period analysis.

177 territories show increasing heat days

The study identifies rising annual days above 28°C WBGT in 177 territories. The overall pattern varies: increases in southern territories coexist with small changes or decreases elsewhere.

Population weighting changes the picture

The study estimates that Oklahoma and Arizona account for about 92% of hazardous-heat person-days, while holding about 77% of the population on the analyzed Native lands. These estimates describe the study’s included population, not all Indigenous people in the United States.

Local changes are larger than the average

The reported mean change is about 0.8 days across 476 territories, but some Oklahoma territories show roughly 8–12 additional hazardous days. A single average conceals substantial regional variation.

Exposure gaps widen in parts of Arizona

The study reports more than 11 additional days in the exposure disparity for some Arizona territories, including Baboquivari. Not every territory follows the same pattern; terrain also matters within the state.

Reading boundaries

Very small land areas can appear as outlines. A territory extending across a state border may appear as a narrow border feature in one state. Those shapes should not be interpreted as missing territories or the absence of exposure.

What the findings can—and cannot—tell us

The maps identify exposure patterns that can guide more detailed adaptation assessments. They do not measure individual heat illness, establish the causal effect of historical policies, or predict future health outcomes. WBGT is modeled using fixed wind speed without direct solar radiation; climate resolution, population weights, and linear trend assumptions affect the estimates.

Counts differ across analysis stages in the source materials: the overview describes 484 evaluated territories, while the trend results use 476. Findings above retain their stated analysis context. Before a formal publication, those totals and the trend-period labels need to be reconciled against a single versioned output.

My main lesson was that geographic identifiers, population weighting, and time windows are central research decisions. Future work could use changing population weights, assess nonlinear trends, and connect modeled exposure with health-outcome data.
